# Brand Strategy and Positioning


---

## Slop Test

If your positioning could apply to any competitor in the category, it's not positioning. It's wallpaper.
If your "differentiation" is a list of adjectives (innovative, trusted, human-centered), you've described nothing.
If your strategy document takes more than one page to explain, you haven't found the simple truth yet.

---

## Brand Definition

"A brand is a person's gut feeling about a product, service, or company."
It's not what YOU say it is. It's what THEY say it is.

---

## The Three Little Questions

Every brand must answer all three. If any answer is weak, you don't have a brand.

1. **Who are you?**
2. **What do you do?**
3. **Why does it matter?**

Most companies struggle with all three. Especially the third.

---

## The Onliness Statement

The true test of a zag. If you can't say you're the "only," go back and start over.

**Basic form:**
"Our [offering] is the ONLY [category] that [benefit]."

**Expanded 6-part form:**
1. **WHAT** — your category
2. **HOW** — how you're different
3. **WHO** — your customers
4. **WHERE** — geographic location
5. **WHEN** — the era or trend you ride
6. **WHY** — why you're important / the need state

**Example:** Harley-Davidson — "The ONLY motorcycle manufacturer that makes big, loud motorcycles for macho guys (and macho wannabees) mostly in the United States who want to join a gang of cowboys in an era of decreasing personal freedom."

Use the onliness statement as a decisional filter. Every decision should help or hurt, focus or unfocus, purify or modify your brand.

---

## The Trueline Hierarchy

Three levels, from strategic to customer-facing:

1. **Onliness Statement** — strategic positioning (internal)
2. **Trueline** — "the one true thing you can say about your brand." Can't be reduced, refuted, or easily dismissed. Your value proposition.
3. **Tagline** — customer-facing expression of the trueline

**Rule:** One proposition per brand. If you need commas or "ands," you need more focus.

| Brand | Trueline | Tagline |
|---|---|---|
| Southwest | Fly anywhere for less than it costs to drive | You're now free to move about the country |
| Mini | The small car for people who want fun driving | Let's motor |
| eBay | The place to trade practically anything on Earth | The world's online marketplace |

## The Good/Different Chart

Two axes: GOOD (quality, aesthetics, price, function) x DIFFERENT (surprising, novel, offbeat).

| Quadrant | Description | Focus Group Result |
|---|---|---|
| Good + Different | Successful zags. Cirque du Soleil, Prius, Schwab | Test poorly (too many negatives) |
| Good + Not Different | Safe but unexciting. No differentiation. | Test extremely well |
| Not Good + Different | Usually fails | |
| Not Good + Not Different | Commodity | Test fairly well (nothing to dislike) |

Real winners in the upper-right quadrant often look like failures in testing. Takes experienced judgment to recognize them.

## The Five Disciplines of Branding

### 1. Differentiate
Focus is the word. An unfocused brand doesn't stand for anything. A focused brand knows exactly what it is, why it's different, and why people want it.

**Focus principle:** Better to be #1 in a small category than #3 in a large one.

### 2. Collaborate
"It takes a village to build a brand." The best model is the Integrated Marketing Team: best-of-breed specialists coordinated internally, brand knowledge stays with the company.

### 3. Innovate
Innovation gives brands traction. Good strategy + poor execution is a Ferrari with flat tires.

**MAYA principle** (Raymond Loewy): Most Advanced Yet Acceptable. Push boundaries without losing the audience.

**The Innovation Test:** "How do you know when an idea is innovative? When it scares the hell out of everybody."

### 4. Validate
Move from one-way broadcast to feedback loop. Test ideas before market with one-on-one interviews and ethnographic observation. Avoid focus groups as decision-making tools.

### 5. Cultivate
Brands are organic. They need care and evolution over time, not static brand books.

## Clarifying Strategy

Phase 2 involves analysis, discovery, synthesis, simplicity, and clarity. The best strategies go where others have not.

**Strategy flow:**
Understanding → Clarifying (core values, brand attributes) → Positioning (differentiation, value proposition) → Brand essence (central idea, unifying concept) → Big Idea

### Narrowing the Focus

Examine current business strategy, core values, target markets, competitors, distribution, and competitive advantage. Look at economic, sociopolitical, and global trends.

"A brand becomes stronger when you narrow the focus."
"As the mass and volume of information increases, people search for a clear signal that gives pattern, shape, and direction to the voice."

### Uncover Brand Essence

Seven questions:
1. What does this company do that is best in the world?
2. Why do customers choose it over competition?
3. What business are they in?
4. How is it really different from its most successful competitor?
5. What are three adjectives that summarize how this company wants to be perceived?
6. What are its strengths and weaknesses?
7. How clear are these answers?

### Positioning

A brand's position is the conceptual territory it claims relative to other brands, informed by customer needs, competition, and brand advantages. Understand the broader cultural backdrop.

"The essence of positioning is sacrifice. You must be willing to give up something to establish that unique position."

### The Onliness Exercise

"OUR [offering] IS THE ONLY [category] THAT [benefit]."

"If you can't say why your brand is both different and compelling in a few words, don't fix your statement. Fix your company."

## Named Rules

### The Zag Principle
"When everybody zigs, zag." You can't be a leader by following. Radical differentiation finds a whole new market space you can own and defend.

### The White Space Rule
Look for where competitors aren't. Think like an artist: appreciate negative space, not just positive. The human perceptual system notices what's there, not what's not there. Train yourself to see gaps.

### The Sacrifice Game
Prune unaligned elements, then add elements that increase focus. If adding an element brings you into competition with a stronger competitor, think twice.

### The Power Law Rule
In mature categories, #1 has roughly 2x the share of #2, which has 2x #3. Only positions #1 and #2 are worth competing for. Below #3, start a new category.

---

## Bans

- Do not describe a brand using only abstract adjectives. Name concrete behaviors, objects, or references.
- Do not position against a competitor you cannot credibly challenge.
- Do not skip the onliness statement. Every brand identity this skill produces must have one.
- Do not confuse a tagline with a strategy. A tagline is the last thing you write, not the first.
- "Empower," "elevate," "seamless," "leverage," "innovative," "cutting-edge" are banned. (ARCHITECTURE.md shared design laws)

---

## Permissions

- You may simplify the onliness statement for solo founders who serve a local market. The WHERE and WHEN fields can be implicit.
- You may iterate the onliness statement across multiple interview rounds. It rarely arrives in the first pass.
- You may use the Good/Different chart as a workshop exercise during the interview phase.
