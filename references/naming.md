# Brand Naming and Taglines


---

## Slop Test

If the name sounds like it was generated by concatenating two Latin roots, it was.
If the tagline could belong to any company in the category, it belongs to none of them.
If you picked the name before doing the interview, you named the wrong thing.

---

## Why Names Matter

"The right name can be a brand's most valuable asset, driving differentiation and speeding acceptance. The wrong name can cost millions in workarounds and lost income over a lifetime."

George Bernard Shaw: "Take care to get born well."

## Seven Types of Brand Names

| Type | Description | Example | Strength | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Founder** | Named after a person | Chanel, Disney, Tesla | Heritage, authority | Hard to transfer, succession issues |
| **Descriptive** | Says what it does | General Motors, PayPal | Immediate clarity | Generic, hard to protect |
| **Fabricated** | Invented word | Kodak, Xerox, Spotify | Highly ownable, protectable | Requires heavy investment to build meaning |
| **Metaphor** | Symbolic reference | Amazon, Nike, Apple | Rich associations, storytelling | Can feel forced if metaphor is stale |
| **Acronym** | Initials | IBM, BMW, UPS | Short, efficient | No meaning until established, no imagery |
| **Magic Spell** | Altered spelling/wordplay | Lyft, Flickr, Tumblr | Distinctive, modern | Can feel gimmicky, spelling confusion |
| **Combination** | Merged words/concepts | Facebook, YouTube, Pinterest | Descriptive + ownable | Can sound clunky |

## The Seven Criteria for a Good Name

Seven criteria for evaluating brand name quality:

### 1. Distinctiveness
Does it stand out from the crowd, especially from other names in its class? The best brand names have the "presence" of a proper noun. It must be different from competitors.

### 2. Brevity
Four syllables or less. Short enough to be easily recalled and used. Will it resist being reduced to non-communicating initials? Long multi-word names get shortened to meaningless acronyms.

### 3. Appropriateness
Is there a reasonable fit with the business purpose? But not so descriptive that it sounds generic. If the name would work just as well for a competitor, keep looking.

### 4. Easy Spelling and Pronunciation
Will most people spell it after hearing it? Pronounce it after reading it? It shouldn't become a spelling test or make people feel ignorant.

### 5. Likability
Will people enjoy using it? Names that are intellectually stimulating or provide good "mouth feel" have a head start. Onomatopoeia is powerful (Smuckers sounds like smacking lips).

### 6. Extendability
Does it have "legs"? Does it suggest visual interpretation or lend itself to creative executions? Great names provide endless opportunities for brandplay.

### 7. Protectability
Can it be trademarked? Is it available for web use? Some names are more legally defensible than others, making them safer and more valuable long-term.

## Naming Process (3 Phases)

### Phase 1: Explore
Generate a wide field of candidates across all seven name types. Use the brand's onliness statement and personality words as creative fuel. Quantity over quality at this stage.

**Techniques:**
- Free association from brand attributes
- Metaphor mining (what objects, places, or experiences embody the brand?)
- Word combination and portmanteau
- Foreign language roots
- Sound symbolism (sharp consonants for precision, soft vowels for comfort)

### Phase 2: Evaluate
Run every candidate through the seven criteria. Score each name 1-5 on each criterion. Names scoring below 3 on any single criterion are cut.

### Phase 3: Validate
Three validation tests:

1. **The Bar Test** — say the name aloud in a noisy room. Can people hear it, understand it, and remember it? Does it survive real-world conditions?
2. **The Radio Test** — hear the name spoken without seeing it written. Can listeners spell it? Do they get the right mental image?
3. **The Competitor Test** — place the name alongside the top 5 competitors. Does it stand out or blend in? Does it occupy different sonic and visual territory?

## High-Imagery vs. Low-Imagery Names

High-imagery names are more memorable than low-imagery names.

| Category | Examples | Memorability |
|---|---|---|
| Greek/Latin roots | Accenture, Innoveda, Agilent | Low imagery |
| Anglo-Saxon / concrete words | Apple, Shell, Amazon | High imagery |
| People names | Betty Crocker, Ben & Jerry's | High imagery |

The most powerful names combine well with visual treatment to create a memorable brand icon.

## Name Architecture

Within a brand system, examine:
- **Core brand name** — the master brand
- **Naming system for products and services** — how sub-brands relate
- **Descriptors and domains** — supporting language

Three portfolio models:
1. **Branded House** — company IS the brand (Apple, Google). Sub-products share the master name.
2. **House of Brands** — separate brand names (P&G owns Tide, Pampers, Gillette). Each fights alone.
3. **Hybrid** — mix of both. Risk: the "muddy middle" where nothing is clear.

## Tagline Principles

### What a Tagline Is
A short phrase that captures the brand's trueline in customer-facing language. It is the last thing you write, not the first.

### The Trueline-to-Tagline Flow

1. Start with the **onliness statement** (strategic)
2. Extract the **trueline** — the one true thing you can say. Can't be reduced, refuted, or dismissed.
3. Polish into a **tagline** — customer-facing, memorable, emotionally resonant

**Rule:** One proposition per brand. If you're using commas or "ands," you need more focus.

### Tagline Quality Checks
- Remove the brand name. Does the tagline still evoke the brand? If it could belong to any competitor, it fails.
- Say it aloud. Does it have rhythm? Good taglines have sonic quality.
- Can it extend into creative executions (ads, social, packaging)? Great taglines are generative, not dead-end.
- Is it honest? A tagline that promises what the brand can't deliver erodes trust.

## The Law of Bisociation

The best brand names connect two previously unrelated ideas or domains. This cognitive collision creates memorability and meaning.

**Examples:**
- **Amazon** — a river + commerce (scale, flow, everything)
- **Apple** — a fruit + technology (approachability, simplicity)
- **Shell** — a seashell + petroleum (origin story, natural form)

The bisociation should feel surprising on first encounter but inevitable in hindsight.

## Named Rules

### The Heritage Name Rule
Heritage names aren't automatically bad. Smuckers, Zeiss, and Disney all work because they carry sound symbolism, cultural associations, or founder mythology. Evaluate heritage names on the seven criteria, not on fashion.

### The Category-First Rule
Before naming, define the category you want to own. The name should claim territory in that category, not describe features. Amazon named the river, not the bookstore.

### The Protectability-First Rule
Never fall in love with a name before it's cleared. Conduct trademark searches early. Commission preliminary knockout searches to remove unavailable marks as early as possible.

---

## Bans

- Do not generate names from Greek/Latin root concatenation as a first instinct. These are low-imagery and forgettable.
- Do not name before the interview and strategy phases are complete.
- Do not present more than 3-5 finalist names. Too many options paralyze decision-making.
- Do not use names that require explanation. If the name needs a footnote, it's too clever.
- Do not default to ".io" domains. Check availability of ".com" first.

---

## Permissions

- You may propose a founder's name if the founder is the product (personal brands, chef-driven restaurants, design studios).
- You may use fabricated names when the category is saturated and all descriptive territory is claimed.
- You may revisit naming after identity design if the visual exploration reveals that the name doesn't work visually.
