# Brand Touchpoints

A touchpoint is any surface where the brand meets someone. Every touchpoint is either reinforcing the identity or eroding it. There is no neutral.

---

## Touchpoint Inventory

### Print and Stationery

| Touchpoint | Components | Application rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Business cards** | Name, title, contact, logo, brand color | Logo lockup: horizontal or badge. Paper stock and finish ARE the brand. A cheap card says cheap brand. |
| **Letterhead** | Logo, address block, typography, margin system | Logo top-left or top-center. Body text in brand typeface. Legal footer if required. |
| **Envelopes** | Logo, return address, optional pattern liner | Logo placement consistent with letterhead. Interior liner is an earned detail — use when budget allows. |
| **Invoices and forms** | Logo, structured data fields, footer | Functional first. Brand typography and color in headers. Don't over-design — clarity is the brand expression here. |
| **Labels and stickers** | Logo, die-cut shape, adhesive quality | Shape itself is a brand signal. Circle, rectangle, custom die-cut each say something different. |

### Collateral and Publications

| Touchpoint | Components | Application rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Brochures / sell sheets** | Cover, spread system, photography, CTA | Grid system from guidelines. Photography follows art direction. Headlines in brand voice. |
| **Presentations** | Title slide, content templates, chart styles, closing slide | Master template enforces brand. Limit slide count — brand discipline includes restraint. |
| **Reports / white papers** | Cover, typography hierarchy, data visualization style | Charts and graphs use brand color palette. Don't default to Excel colors. |
| **Printed advertising** | Headline, visual, logo placement, CTA | Logo bottom-right is convention, not law. Placement should serve the ad's visual hierarchy. |

### Digital

| Touchpoint | Components | Application rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Website** | Navigation, typography, color, imagery, interaction patterns, favicon | Favicon = symbol-only mark. Typography must use web-safe fallbacks in CSS stack. |
| **Social media** | Avatar, cover image, post templates, bio, highlights | Avatar = symbol-only mark. Templates maintain brand in the platform's visual noise. |
| **Email** | Signature, newsletter template, transactional templates | Signature: name, title, logo, one link. Newsletter: brand header, clean body, clear unsubscribe. |
| **App / mobile** | Icon, splash screen, UI components, notification style | App icon = symbol mark at its most compressed. Must read at 29pt (iOS smallest). |
| **Video / motion** | Intro/outro, lower thirds, transitions, thumbnail style | Motion has timing. Define easing curves, duration ranges, and transition types. |
| **Audio / podcast** | Intro sound, background music, host voice guidelines | If the brand has sonic identity, it starts here. If not, keep it minimal and consistent. |

### Environmental and Spatial

| Touchpoint | Components | Application rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Signage** | Exterior building sign, directional signs, regulatory signs | Exterior sign is the largest reproduction of the mark. Test legibility at intended viewing distance. |
| **Wayfinding** | Directional system, floor graphics, room identification | Must work without color (accessibility). Iconography over text where possible. |
| **Retail / point of sale** | Window displays, shelf talkers, counter cards, receipts | The receipt is a touchpoint. So is the shopping bag. Brand these. |
| **Office / workspace** | Wall graphics, meeting room names, welcome desk, screen savers | Internal branding matters. Employees are brand ambassadors — surround them with it. |
| **Trade shows / events** | Booth design, banners, badges, swag, handouts | Booth must communicate brand from 20 feet. One message, one visual, one CTA. |
| **Branded environments** | Flagship stores, showrooms, experience centers | Architecture IS the brand. Materials, lighting, sound, scent — all designed. |

### Product and Packaging

| Touchpoint | Components | Application rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Primary packaging** | Product container, label, closure, structural design | Shelf impact: identifiable from 5 feet. Logo placement follows structural hierarchy. |
| **Secondary packaging** | Outer box, shipping carton, tissue, inserts | Unboxing is a touchpoint. Every layer the customer peels is a brand moment or a missed one. |
| **Product design** | Physical form, material choice, color application | The product IS the brand's most important artifact. If it's ugly or cheap, no identity system saves it. |
| **Tags and hangtags** | Material, print method, attachment | Tactile quality matters. A woven label vs a printed one vs a leather patch — each tells a different story. |

### People and Service

| Touchpoint | Components | Application rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Uniforms and workwear** | Garments, accessories, name badges, PPE | Uniforms range from T-shirts to tailored suits. Match the brand's formality. Embroidery for logos — screen printing degrades. |
| **Name badges** | Name, role, logo, attachment method | First point of human-brand contact. Legible from 3 feet. |
| **Customer service scripts** | Greeting, hold message, closing, escalation language | Voice guidelines apply here too. Scripts should sound human, not robotic. |

### Vehicles and Transit

| Touchpoint | Components | Application rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Fleet vehicles** | Cars, vans, trucks — full wrap or partial brand application | Full wrap: maximum impact, higher cost, needs professional design for vehicle curves. Partial: logo + URL + color panels, more versatile. |
| **Delivery vehicles** | Branded for the moment of arrival | The delivery vehicle is mobile advertising and the customer's first physical brand contact. |
| **Specialty / novelty** | Bicycles, scooters, drones, boats, aircraft | If the brand uses unusual transport, brand it. It becomes memorable specifically because it's unexpected. |

### Merchandise and Promotional

| Touchpoint | Components | Application rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Branded merchandise** | Apparel, bags, notebooks, drinkware, tech accessories | The test: would someone use this if it didn't have a logo on it? If no, the merch is a waste. |
| **Promotional items** | Pens, stickers, pins, lanyards, temporary tattoos | Cheap swag damages brand perception. Either do it well or don't. |
| **Gifts and awards** | Client gifts, employee awards, milestone objects | Special objects carry disproportionate brand weight. Invest here. |

## Rollout Tiers

Not every brand needs every touchpoint from day one. Match the rollout to the brand's stage and budget.

### Minimum Rollout (launch-ready)
- [ ] Logo files in all formats (SVG, PNG, EPS)
- [ ] Business card (print-ready)
- [ ] Email signature
- [ ] Social media avatar + cover image
- [ ] Website with brand typography and color applied
- [ ] One presentation template

### Standard Rollout (funded / growing)
Everything in Minimum, plus:
- [ ] Full stationery suite (letterhead, envelope, invoice)
- [ ] Social media post templates (3-5 formats)
- [ ] Newsletter template
- [ ] Brochure or sell sheet template
- [ ] Branded slide deck
- [ ] Signage specifications
- [ ] Brand guidelines PDF

### Full Rollout (established / enterprise)
Everything in Standard, plus:
- [ ] Packaging system
- [ ] Environmental / retail design standards
- [ ] Vehicle livery specifications
- [ ] Uniform / workwear standards
- [ ] Merchandise guidelines
- [ ] Motion / animation standards
- [ ] Sonic branding
- [ ] Digital component library
- [ ] Brand portal for asset distribution

## Application Principles

These hold true across every touchpoint:

1. **Context before consistency.** A business card and a billboard serve different purposes. The brand should be recognizable across both, but the execution adapts to the medium's constraints and opportunities.

2. **The logo is not always the hero.** On product packaging, the product is the hero. On a website, the content is the hero. The logo confirms the brand — it doesn't need to dominate every surface.

3. **Brand the moments that matter.** The receipt, the hold music, the 404 page, the shipping notification email — these overlooked moments are where brand love is built or lost.

4. **Materials are messages.** Uncoated paper says something different than gloss. Brass says something different than plastic. The material IS a brand decision.

5. **Design for the worst case.** Fax machines, newspaper print, low-res screens, direct sunlight, wet conditions. If the brand must work there, design for there.

**Named rule — The Orphan Test:** Pick up any single touchpoint in isolation — no context, no explanation. Can someone identify the brand? If not, the system is not working hard enough at that point of contact.

**Named rule — The Consistency Stress Test:** The same brand identity must work across the smallest touchpoint (favicon at 16×16px) and the largest (billboard at 48×14ft) without losing recognition. The logo, color ratios, and spatial grammar must encode the brand at all extremes. If an element doesn't scale, it's the wrong element.

**Named rule — The Context-First Principle:** A business card and a billboard serve different purposes. The brand should be recognizable across both, but the execution adapts to the medium's constraints and opportunities. Context rules consistency — not vice versa.

**Named rule — The Material-As-Message Principle:** Uncoated paper says something different than gloss. Brass says something different than plastic. Every material choice IS a brand decision. The identity system includes material specifications, not just digital color values.

## Bans

Match-and-refuse. If a touchpoint design contains any of these, rework before delivery.

| Ban | Why |
|---|---|
| Designing any touchpoint in isolation from the system | A business card that doesn't share spatial grammar with the website isn't a system — it's a collection of artifacts. |
| Logo at sizes below minimum clearance | Every brand has a minimum reproduction size. Below it, the mark dissolves. Define it explicitly. |
| "Branding" that is a logo placed on a generic template | A branded envelope isn't a logo on a white rectangle. The material, color field, pattern, and typography all convey the brand. |
| Overdesigned functional surfaces (invoices, forms, receipts) | Clarity is the brand expression here. If the recipient can't find the total, the design failed — no matter how on-brand it looks. |
| Skipping the worst-case test | If the touchpoint must work on fax machines, low-res screens, or in direct sunlight, design for those conditions. Don't design for ideal and hope. |
| Cheap materials on brand-critical items | A business card on thin gloss stock says "we don't value our first impression." Either invest in the material or skip the item. |

## Touchpoint Slop Test

If you can swap the brand name on a touchpoint and it still looks right for a different brand, it failed.

**Check 1 — The Name-Swap Test:** Remove the brand name from a business card. Replace it with a competitor's name. Does the card still feel right? If yes, the touchpoint has no brand encoding beyond the name.

**Check 2 — The Scale Extremes Test:** View the favicon at 16×16px alongside the billboard at full scale. Is the brand recognizable at both extremes? If either end fails, the visual system has scale gaps.

**Check 3 — The Isolation Test:** (The Orphan Test, formalized.) Show a single touchpoint to someone with no context. Can they name the brand? If not, rework that touchpoint.

**Check 4 — The Material Reality Check:** If the brand is "craft" but every touchpoint is a flat digital surface, there is a gap between what the brand says it is and what the materials show. Materials carry brand weight — use them.

**Check 5 — The Worst-Case Test:** Convert the touchpoint to greyscale at newspaper-dot resolution. Is it still readable and on-brand? If not, the design relies on conditions that won't always be met.
